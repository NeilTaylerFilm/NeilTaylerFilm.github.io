## Context
During my final year of university, our graduate project was to create a short film. We launched a crowdfunding campaign to raise money for the project. As one of the perks for donating, backers would receive access to a Vanity Fair inspired explainer video, where the director of the film would go through key scenes from the film, providing annotations and commentary along the way.

![](https://neiltaylerfilm-images.neiltayler2003.workers.dev/photos/7a5658be9689bc32fe555914fca75e16-2560.jpeg)

I edited this video in Adobe Premiere Pro. I've found myself as of late bouncing between Adobe Premiere Pro and DaVinci Resolve. Years ago, I had abandoned Adobe Premiere Pro in favour of DaVinci Resolve. However, I've recently seen many companies and freelancers relying on an Adobe centred workflow, which inspired me to revisit Premiere Pro.

I had never forgotten how to use Premiere Pro, in fact, this venture was a reminder of how pleasant it can be to edit in Premiere. There's virtually a keyboard shortcut for everything, which really allowed me to blitz through the timeline.

## Configuration
For this video, there were 3 camera angles (a wide, an off angle close up and a top down shot) plus audio from lavalier and shotgun microphones, captured on an external audio recorder.

The audio was recorded onto a single stereo track, with the shotgun occupying the left channel and the lavalier occupying the right channel. I opted to use only audio from the lavalier microphone, the shotgun was placed quite far from the talent, resulting in a lot of reverb. Performing de-reverb on the shotgun audio would’ve been more effort than it was worth. I collapsed the audio down to a mono configuration, so only audio from the lavalier would come through.

I created a multicam sequence and synced the audio. We weren't able to use timecode for this shoot, so I had to sync the scratch audio from all the angles to the audio from the external recorder. Creating a multicam sequence for this edit payed dividends throughout the edit as I could easily switch between the different angles with just two keyboard shortcuts.

![](https://neiltaylerfilm-images.neiltayler2003.workers.dev/photos/b8f2bb19219ed10aeb8951918c87506a-2560.jpeg)

## Colour Correction
I performed the colour correction on the source footage level rather than on a clip basis, meaning I only needed to perform the colour correction once. If I needed make modifications to the colour of a clip later down the line, I could make the adjustment to the source footage and it would ripple to every instance of the clip in the timeline.

![](https://neiltaylerfilm-images.neiltayler2003.workers.dev/photos/09b3725163270dc95179a5d300b59eaf-2560.jpeg)

Fortunately, we had a Datacolor Spyder Checkr 24 on set, so I made sure to present this to all camera angles when clapping. That's me holding the colour checker. This was one of the benefits to being able to be on set. I was able to prepare for post-production during filming.

We captured using two Sony FS7 cameras and one DJI Osmo Pocket 3. Using the HueVsHue curves, I was able to make sure the colours on the colour checker lines up with the vectorscope.

For a colour critical project, I might've preferred to work in DaVinci Resolve. However, I knew given the nature of the project that advanced colour grading wasn't necessary, the whole video could be completed in Premiere Pro. (I'm very curious to see how the new Colour tab in the Premiere Beta performs.)

I developed a skin tone indicator LUT that I used on this project to help me identify at a glance whether the skin tones leant too green or magenta. This also helped match skin tones between the different cameras.

![](https://neiltaylerfilm-images.neiltayler2003.workers.dev/photos/6c2b7f302f4a2468d35b1423f30c420e-2560.jpeg)

## Audio Processing
This is where my background in music production came in handy. I processed the lavalier audio using several effects; EQ, compression, de-noise, limiters and more.

![](https://neiltaylerfilm-images.neiltayler2003.workers.dev/photos/ab6cb5dfb44773217792a41af79dd741-2560.jpeg)

I used this project to explore Adobe's Essential Sound panel. I think I still prefer to work in the Audio Track Mixer since I've acquired many VSTs that I like to use but I appreciate having many necessary tools in one place.

## Motion Graphics
I frequently found throughout the video that on-screen graphics were needed to aid in the director's explanation on certain topics. I took the liberty of creating a few motion graphics myself, like this one which I used to help explain how backdrops were moved around the camera during a fight scene.

![](https://neiltaylerfilm-images.neiltayler2003.workers.dev/photos/2b4be334c687b83f4544ae4e7eec2d1f-2560.jpeg)

I built this motion graphic in its own sequence, which I then referenced in the main sequence, as shown below.

![](https://neiltaylerfilm-images.neiltayler2003.workers.dev/photos/13b16ec7579ac1187c2fbb1cb0f60c71-2560.jpeg)

Other graphics, I was able to generate utilising AI. I wired ChatGPT's Codex to the Hyperframes plugin, which allows it to turn .html files into .mov sequences.

To be clear, I generally despise AI and there are few circumstances where I find myself turning to AI, especially in the editing process. However, I can also admit where it has its uses.

![](https://neiltaylerfilm-images.neiltayler2003.workers.dev/photos/4e770a2952b1527f9c7cbd3918d5ce62-2560.jpeg)

I could've absolutely hand-made the graphics that I used AI to generate. However, I saved a significant amount of time by not having to design bespoke graphics in Photoshop or Illustrator and animate those. Not to mention, I was able to continue cutting while the graphics were being generated in the background.
