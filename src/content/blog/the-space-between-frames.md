An image begins before the shutter is pressed. There is the walk, the pause, the small adjustment to where you stand. The frame is only the last part of a much longer process of paying attention. Leaving a little room for that process can change what you notice, and what you choose to keep.

## A reason to slow down

This is a sample article for the journal. Its subject is looking: not for a spectacular scene, but for the ordinary things that become interesting when given a little time. A reflection. Two lines meeting. A person briefly crossing a patch of light.

The photograph above is a sample from Unsplash, not Neil’s work. It demonstrates how a landscape image sits alongside writing without turning every entry into a portfolio piece.

> Not every photograph has to explain a place. Sometimes it is enough to suggest how it felt to be there.

### What belongs in the frame?

Try looking at the edges before the centre. A bright corner can pull attention away from what first made the scene interesting. Moving a step sideways can be more useful than changing a lens.

- Find the thing that made you stop.
- Watch how the light moves around it.
- Look at what the frame leaves out.
- Make a photograph, then make time to look again.

## Looking at the edit

A contact sheet changes the question. Instead of asking whether a single photograph works, you start asking what the images say together. Two quiet photographs might make more sense beside each other than either does alone.

Give an edit a little distance. A photograph you remember making can carry a significance that a new viewer cannot see. Time helps separate the experience of taking it from the experience of looking at it.
